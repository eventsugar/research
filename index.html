What is Research?

Research is a systematic, organized process of gathering, analyzing, and interpreting information to answer questions, solve problems, or gain new knowledge.

Key Characteristics

  • Purpose-driven — focused on a specific problem or question
  • Systematic — follows a structured plan (plan → collect → analyze → report)
  • Objective — based on evidence, not personal opinion
  • Evidence-based — conclusions drawn from data
  • Critical thinking — requires analysis and synthesis

Types of Research

TypeDescription
Basic/FundamentalBuilds general knowledge; no immediate application
AppliedSolves real-world, practical problems
DescriptiveDescribes phenomena without manipulating variables
ExploratoryInvestigates new or underexplored areas
AnalyticalInterprets data to find patterns or causes

Importance of Research in IT

IT research plays a vital role in development, improvement, and effective use of modern technologies.

1. Drives Innovation

  • New tech: AI, cloud, IoT, cybersecurity, data science

2. Improves Problem Solving

  • Evidence-based solutions to systems/network/software issues

3. Supports Decision-Making

  • Reliable data for tech selection and IT investment

4. Enhances System Quality

  • Improves performance, reliability, scalability, security

5. Strengthens Cybersecurity

  • Identifies threats, vulnerabilities, attack patterns

6. Improves Efficiency

  • Reduces costs, improves automation and productivity

7. Academic/Professional Growth

  • Keeps professionals current with emerging trends

8. Addresses Societal Needs

  • Healthcare, education, finance, government services

9. Evidence-Based Practices

  • Systems built on tested data, not assumptions

10. Prepares for Future Tech

  • Anticipates challenges, ensures digital readiness
Example: Research on cloud security led to encryption protocols that protect sensitive data, allowing businesses to confidently adopt cloud services.

Defining Research Problems & Questions

The Research Problem

A broad issue the researcher aims to explore or solve. It forms the foundation of the entire study.

Steps to Define the Research Problem

1
Choose a broad area of interest you want to explore
2
Conduct a literature review to find gaps
3
Narrow down to a specific, focused problem
4
Check feasibility — can it be done in the time/resources available?

A well-defined problem is: Clearly stated · Specific · Feasible · Relevant · Addresses a knowledge gap

Research Questions

More specific than the problem — break it into manageable parts. Guide data collection and analysis.

Types of Research Questions

TypeExample
Descriptive"What are the challenges faced by small businesses in urban areas?"
Comparative"How does customer satisfaction differ between online and in-store shopping?"
Causal"What impact does social media have on teenage self-esteem?"

A well-defined question is: Clear · Focused · Feasible · Significant · Answerable through empirical data

Literature Review

A critical component that establishes the foundation for research by reviewing existing knowledge, theories, and technologies related to the topic.

Why Do a Literature Review?

  • Identify research gaps — find what has not been explored
  • Build on existing knowledge — place new research in context
  • Clarify theories and concepts — define key terms and frameworks
  • Establish methodologies — learn commonly used approaches
  • Provide rationale — justify why your study is needed

Structure of a Literature Review

a
Introduction — purpose, scope, research questions
b
Thematic or Chronological Organization — group by topic OR timeline
c
Summary & Synthesis — key findings, critical analysis, comparisons
d
Identify Gaps — unresolved issues, limitations, emerging trends
e
Conclusion — summary of findings, gaps, implications for future research

Sources to Use

Academic Journals Conference Proceedings Books & Book Chapters Technical Reports Theses & Dissertations Online Resources (use cautiously)

Data Collection Methods

The process of gathering information to answer research questions or test hypotheses.

8 Key Methods

MethodData TypeTools / Notes
Surveys/QuestionnairesQuantitativeGoogle Forms, SurveyMonkey; large samples; Likert scale
InterviewsQualitativeStructured / Semi / Unstructured; Zoom, in-person
ObservationsBothParticipant vs. Non-participant
ExperimentsQuantitativeA/B testing, lab simulations
Case StudiesQualitativeDeep study of one case over time
Focus GroupsQualitativeGroup discussions with specific users/professionals
Secondary DataBothDatabases, journals, system logs
Analytics & LogsQuantitativeGoogle Analytics, server logs

Steps in Data Collection

1
Define the research problem
2
Choose collection method(s)
3
Design data collection tools
4
Select participants
5
Collect data
6
Analyze the data
7
Report findings

Key Considerations

  • Privacy & Security — anonymize data, use encryption
  • Validity & Reliability — accurate and repeatable results
  • Sampling — representative sample ensures generalizability
  • Bias — use randomized selection to reduce sampling bias
  • Ethics — informed consent; explain data usage to participants

Observation as a Data Collection Tool

Watching and recording behaviors/events in real-time. Used to study user interactions with software, hardware, or IT systems.

Types of Observation

Structured

  • Predefined categories (e.g., counting button clicks, task time)

Unstructured

  • Open recording; good for complex or unknown behaviors

Participant

  • Researcher actively joins in and observes from within

Non-participant

  • Researcher observes from a distance without involvement

11-Step Observation Process

1
Define problem & objectives — clarify focus, metrics (task time, error rate, etc.)
2
Identify subjects & environment — who and where
3
Determine observation method — structured/unstructured/participant/non-participant
4
Prepare checklist/guide — template for recording actions, system responses, user reactions
5
Set up session — time, location, equipment, pilot test
6
Conduct observation — record key points; remain neutral
7
Record data — audio/video, notes, screen recordings
8
Analyze data — transcribe, categorize, find patterns
9
Interpret findings — synthesize, contextualize, compare with literature
10
Report findings — summarize, visualize, recommend
11
Ethical considerations — consent, confidentiality, non-intrusive approach

Research Proposal Writing

A structured plan that presents your research idea in an organized and convincing way.

10 Components of a Research Proposal

#ComponentWhat to Include
1TitleClear, specific, reflective of the topic
2IntroductionBackground, context, problem statement
3Research ObjectivesSpecific, measurable, achievable goals
4Literature ReviewExisting work, gaps, how yours differs
5MethodologyResearch design, data collection, analysis, tools
6Expected ResultsAnticipated outcomes and impact
7Timeline & Work PlanMilestones by month (Gantt chart style)
8Budget & ResourcesSoftware, hardware, hosting, etc.
9Ethical ConsiderationsConsent, anonymization, data privacy
10ConclusionSummary of proposal and its significance

Tips for a Strong Proposal

  • Clarity & conciseness — avoid unnecessary jargon
  • Logical structure — easy to follow flow
  • Feasibility — realistic given time, budget, resources
  • Relevance — addresses a significant current problem
  • Originality — clearly state what is new about your work

Research Report Structure

Communicates research results in a structured, systematic manner. Degree level = 6 chapters.

SectionContent
Title PageTitle, researcher, institution, supervisor, date
Abstract150–300 words: problem, objectives, methods, findings, conclusion
Ch. 1: IntroductionBackground, problem statement, objectives, research questions, scope, significance
Ch. 2: Literature ReviewExisting research, comparisons, gaps, how your study builds on past work
Ch. 3: MethodologyResearch design, data collection, tools, sampling, analysis, ethics
Ch. 4: System Design/ImplementationRequirements, architecture, tools, design diagrams (DFD, ERD)
Ch. 5: Results & FindingsData presentation (charts, tables), interpretation, patterns/anomalies
Ch. 6: Conclusion & RecommendationsSummary, answers to research questions, recommendations, future research
ReferencesAPA-formatted list of all cited sources
AppendicesQuestionnaires, raw data, code, documentation
Methodology section must include: Research design · Participants · Data collection methods · Analysis techniques · Ethical considerations

Research Methodology (Chapter 3 Content)

Research Design

  • Descriptive design is most common
  • Explains overall strategy for the study

Study Area

  • Where the research takes place and why it was chosen

Target Population

  • Staff, users, administrators, management
  • Those directly involved with the current system

Sampling

  • Purposive — key informants with knowledge
  • Simple random — other respondents

Data Collection

  • Primary: Interviews, Questionnaires, Observation
  • Secondary: Documents, journals, reports, books

Data Analysis

  • Qualitative — interpret interviews and observations; understand experiences
  • Quantitative — tables, charts, percentages from questionnaires

SDLC — System Development Life Cycle

1
System Analysis — Identify problems, determine user requirements
2
System Design — Architecture, database, interface design
3
System Development — Write code, develop components
4
System Testing — Find and correct errors
5
System Implementation — Deploy for actual use
6
System Maintenance — Update and improve after deployment

Ethical Considerations

  • Informed consent — participants know the purpose
  • Voluntary participation
  • Confidentiality — data used only for academic purposes
  • Permission from relevant authorities before starting

Section 3: CybersecurityThreats, data privacy, recent security tools
Section 4: IT InfrastructureOn-premise vs. cloud, scalability, multi-cloud environments
Section 5: ChallengesDaily challenges, resource management, problem-solving examples
Section 6: Future of ITNext decade trends, skill development, education gaps

Qualitative vs. Quantitative

QualitativeQuantitative
DataWords, descriptions, experiencesNumbers, statistics
MethodsInterviews, observations, case studiesSurveys, experiments, logs
GoalUnderstand experiences and contextMeasure and generalize
AnalysisThematic codingStatistical analysis

Primary vs. Secondary Data

Primary Data

  • Collected firsthand by the researcher
  • Interviews, questionnaires, observations
  • More relevant and specific to your study

Secondary Data

  • Already existing data from other sources
  • Books, journals, databases, reports
  • Supports and validates primary findings
